There is a moment, right after you write something down, where you feel a small release of tension. The thing is no longer rattling around in your head. It's on the list. Handled.

That feeling is the trap.

It's not that the feeling is fake. The thing *is* out of your head, and that's useful: working memory is finite, and offloading is real relief. The trap is that the relief feels like progress, and it isn't. You haven't decided anything. You haven't done anything. You've moved an item from one queue (your mind) to another queue (your app), and the second queue is bigger and less forgiving than the first.

## The asymmetry

Capture is cheap. It takes two seconds and costs no commitment. You can capture a hundred things in a week without breaking stride.

Decision is expensive. It takes attention, comparison, and a kind of commitment that capture never demands. To decide is to kill ninety-nine options in order to do one. That's the cognitive cost, and it doesn't go down by being deferred. It compounds.

So a list that grows without a matching decision practice isn't a productivity system. It's a backlog of unmade decisions, and the longer it sits the heavier each one gets. The six-month-old task you keep carrying forward isn't a task anymore. It's a small daily reminder that you have a decision you're avoiding.

## Triage is the missing step

What almost nobody does, and what serious methodologies from GTD onward center on, is a regular triage step between capture and action. Not doing the work. Just deciding, item by item, what each captured thing *is*.

- Is this a task, or a project pretending to be one?
- Is this mine, or did I capture someone else's commitment?
- Is this now, this week, someday, or never?
- Will I ever do this?

The last question is the one people skip, and it's the one that matters. A capture-first app never asks it. The list grows because asking is uncomfortable and the app has no opinion. Triage is the practice of asking anyway, regularly, until the list reflects what you'll do instead of everything you feared forgetting.

## Why people skip it

It feels like overhead. You sat down to *do work*, not to fondle your list. Triage has none of the dopamine of capture and none of the satisfaction of completion. It lives in the dull middle, and its reward, a shorter list that reflects reality, is invisible until you've done it a few times.

It also requires admitting things. Triage means looking at a task you captured in March and acknowledging it's never going to happen, then deleting it. That's a small grief. Most people would rather carry the task forever than have the conversation. So the list becomes a graveyard of former intentions, and every time you open the app you skim past the headstones.

## A working practice

This is what a calm triage loop looks like, and it doesn't need an app to enforce it, only a habit.

1. **Capture freely.** Don't triage at capture time. That's a different mode and mixing them kills both. Just get it out of your head.
2. **Triage on a cadence, not on a whim.** Daily is too often for most people; weekly is enough. The point is that it's *scheduled*, not reactive.
3. **Decide what each thing is, not when you'll do it.** Task or project. Now or someday. Mine or not. The "when" is a separate decision that the doing will reveal.
4. **Delete ruthlessly.** The someday list can acknowledge what's never going to happen. "Maybe" is a valid bucket. "Pretending I'll do this" is not.

The reward isn't a perfect list. It's a list you trust, which means a list you can stop thinking about, which means a head that's quiet enough to do the one thing in front of you.

Capture is free. That's exactly why it's not the part that matters.
